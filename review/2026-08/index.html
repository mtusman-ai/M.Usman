Signal to Decision: August 2026

The shifting AI stack: risks and realities How changes in AI infrastructure alter the choices facing business leaders

Five moves, and what each asks of you.

⦿ Act on routing. Put it on the architecture diagram and in the vendor register.

⦿ Act on open weights. Use the open baseline when pricing renewals; update breach scenarios for autonomous attack tooling.

⦿ Act on the right to check. Enforcement has begun. Refresh the legal assessment and the board agenda.

⦿ Treat benchmark headlines cautiously. Jalapeño's multiples are OpenAI's own results; the second-source question matters more.

⦿ Protect the meaning layer. Context now sits under neutral governance. Decide whether you need structure, memory, or both.

The bottom line for the business

AI developments, enterprise architecture and business strategy are no longer separate conversations. They are one operating question: who controls the layers between your data, your applications and the intelligence they use?

There is a box between an application and the models it calls. Most teams put it there deliberately: it routes requests, applies policy and keeps suppliers interchangeable. I have built systems around that pattern. In August, the box acquired a prospective new owner. Elsewhere, model weights became more available, custom silicon moved closer to the model provider, enforcement gained practical force, and the standards beneath agent context shifted into neutral hands.

Five layers moved. Three gained a named controller. Two did not. The second fact is the more useful one: the dependencies you cannot name are the dependencies you cannot negotiate.

The numbers that matter

⦿ More than $7bn: the reported price for the company that routes model requests.

⦿ 3% or €15m: the maximum fine under the AI Act's Article 101 for a general-purpose AI-model provider, where the article applies.

⦿ 30bn parameters: a permissively licensed model sized for a single office graphics card.

Signal 1: Model routing is becoming a strategic dependency

Stripe has agreed to acquire OpenRouter. Stripe confirmed the proposed deal; the price was not disclosed. Bloomberg, reported by TechCrunch, put it at more than $7bn. A higher $7.5bn figure has circulated, but I cannot trace that figure to a primary source, so I have not used it.

OpenRouter is an intermediary layer. Instead of integrating separately with every model supplier, a team sends requests through one gateway that can route them according to price, capability, availability and policy. I know why teams choose it: one connection removes years of integration work. It also concentrates a dependency.

The transaction has not closed, so there is not yet a new owner. There is, however, a new question for leadership: if this layer changes commercial terms or gives ninety days' notice, what is the practical route around it? I would test that exit path before agreeing any new commercial term.

There are many other open-source routers available today. I have watched this cycle repeat: a technology concept arrives through an open-source foundation, gains traction, and is then commercialised. Building AI systems needs reliability, consistency and adaptability, and the stack is moving quickly, with the ownership model moving alongside it.

In your seat

⦿ CIO: draw routing into the technology estate this quarter, test the exit path, and price an in-house route.

⦿ CFO: record routing as a vendor dependency before renewal season, not as an invisible part of model spend.

Signal 2: Open weights change the negotiating position

One term, plainly: weights. A weight is a value a model learned during training. The collection of weights is the model itself. When weights are released, the capability can be run outside the original provider's service.

Meta released Muse Glimmer on 10 August under Apache 2.0: 30 billion parameters, compressed to run on a 24GB card. Sixteen days later, Z.ai released GLM-5.3-Flash under the MIT licence: 320 billion parameters in total, with 18 billion active for a token.

The active figure is a useful guide to per-token compute. It is not the whole hardware story: the full model still determines the memory and storage needed to load it. Read both numbers before accepting a claim that a model will fit your estate.

Both releases use permissive licences. That does not make deployment free, risk-free or unrestricted in practice; it does give procurement a credible alternative against which to test a managed-service renewal.

How to choose: own it, rent it, or deliberately combine both

The right question is not simply build versus buy. It is which workloads merit control, and which benefit from rented capacity. Consider service-continuity terms, model restrictions, data handling, demand volatility and the cost of a useful result.

Cost per million tokens measures consumption. It does not measure business value. Calculate cost per good answer (CPGA) for a real workflow with the business owner and infrastructure team: include model charges, hosting, human review, rework and the value lost when the answer is not usable. That is the number a renewal must beat. I would take that comparison to the budget meeting.

Own when demand is steady, the workload is sufficiently understood and the data should remain in your estate. Rent when demand is volatile or a workload needs capability you cannot yet operate yourself. I have seen estates arrive at this split without deciding to. Most mature estates need both, but should arrive there by design rather than accident.

In your seat

⦿ CIO: benchmark one production-shaped workload against an open baseline before the next renewal; measure CPGA, not tokens alone.

⦿ CFO: price the renewal against that baseline, including delivery and review costs, before the supplier sets the reference point.

⦿ CSO: revisit any sales proposition built chiefly on a model advantage; the capability floor has moved.

The risk beside openness: agent identity

Taiwan's Ministry of Digital Affairs said that overseas actors reportedly used open-source AI-agent tooling to assemble an autonomous hacking tool. Its monitoring unit detected the campaign in July, issued alerts from 20 July and confirmed the case on 13 August. The ministry did not disclose the origin. The point is not that open weights caused the incident; it is that capable tooling is now easier to assemble into an attack chain.

Eleven days later, Okta announced first-class identity for AI agents. Its Cross App Access capability can give a compatible agent a directory identity and short-lived tokens instead of a stored credential. Those controls are included in core single sign-on plans, but they still require suitable integration, configuration and monitoring. I would not treat product availability as implementation.

In your seat

⦿ CLO: make sure breach scenarios and notification language allow for autonomous attacker capability.

⦿ COO: decide which automated processes may continue during an intrusion and which must fail safe.

⦿ CIO: assess agent identities, token lifetimes and access policies across the agent frameworks you operate.

Signal 3: A model provider is moving further into the stack

The weights are becoming easier to obtain. The silicon beneath inference is becoming more strategic.

OpenAI says its Astra model solved ten open problems in mathematics and theoretical computer science, and that it published machine-checkable proof certificates. I read the repository, rather than relying on coverage. That establishes what the repository claims; it does not establish that every proof verifies.

I did not compile it. The decisive check traverses the dependency graph and requires a build my hardware could not complete. I can describe the published material and its limits, but I will not turn that into a claim of independent verification.

On 25 August, OpenAI published first benchmark results for Jalapeño, its custom inference chip with Broadcom. OpenAI reported 1.5–1.9 times higher work per watt and 1.7–3.6 times lower end-to-end latency than Nvidia GB200 and GB300 systems; it reported a 2.1–4.1 times range on agentic workloads. The tests use a public harness and were presented at Hot Chips, but the results remain vendor-disclosed, not independently reproduced.

The strategic point is not the headline multiple. I would not use vendor results alone to choose a chip. A model provider is also developing the infrastructure that determines the price and availability of its inference. That is a supply-chain question as much as a technical one. I would use it to reopen the second-source question.

In your seat

⦿ CIO: ask suppliers what their inference runs on, what can change without notice and how it affects your 2027 price.

⦿ CSCO: begin second-source qualification for AI compute now, including the operational route to use it.

⦿ CEO: set the level of strategic dependency on any one AI supplier that the business is prepared to carry.

Signal 4: AI Act enforcement now has practical force

Three layers moved without an external referee. The fourth gained one.

On 2 August, the Commission and the AI Office gained enforcement powers for the AI Act's general-purpose AI-model rules. Those obligations had applied since August 2025. Article 50 transparency duties also became enforceable on that date, subject to the regulation's transition arrangements. The change is not that every business suddenly has a filing to make; it is that the authority responsible for enforcement can now act.

Article 101 sets a maximum fine for an in-scope general-purpose AI-model provider at 3% of worldwide annual turnover or €15m, whichever is higher. It is a statutory maximum, not an automatic exposure figure, and it does not automatically apply to every customer or deployer using a model.

Several outlets reported that OpenAI dissolved its centralised Preparedness team and moved catastrophic-risk reviews elsewhere; OpenAI disputes the description. Separately, OpenAI said in early August that it slowed Astra's development after its own evaluation raised cyber-capability concerns. Treat the first as an unresolved report and the second as a company disclosure, not as independent assurance.

For two years, my honest answer to who checked these models was: mostly, the companies themselves. A regulator now sits behind part of that answer.

For UK readers, the Act is not confined to contractual pressure from EU customers: a non-EU provider can be directly in scope where outputs are used in the Union. For Gulf-based businesses, the question is also architectural: self-hosted weights and a subscription service create different obligations, controls and evidence trails. Obtain jurisdiction-specific legal advice before deciding which applies. I would ask that question before committing a product roadmap.

In your seat

⦿ Chairman: put model-risk governance on the board agenda and ask what independent assurance supports management's report.

⦿ CLO: update the legal assessment for the roles the organisation actually performs: provider, deployer, importer, distributor or customer.

⦿ ED: confirm that the AI inventory, accountability and evidence trail are complete before making any regulatory representation.

Signal 5: Agent intelligence has two distinct foundations

The final layer is the one that gives an agent meaning, and it is the layer I know best.

Google's agent-to-agent (A2A) protocol joined the Agentic AI Foundation, placing the communication standard under neutral governance. OpenMetadata 2.0 made an open-source catalogue available as a context layer through the Model Context Protocol (MCP). Together, the changes make it easier for an agent to ask what a table means before acting on it.

An agent that cannot retrieve a definition will infer one. I have spent much of my career on the downstream side of data systems, where a missing definition becomes someone else's operational problem. An agent is only as reliable as its context, onboarding, permissions and access to the organisation's knowledge. Faster compute simply makes a bad inference arrive faster. I would treat an undefined term as a control gap, not a data-cleaning nuisance.

Structure and memory are not the same thing

An ontology gives an agent a shared definition of the terms that matter to the business. A company brain preserves what the organisation has decided, who decided it and when that decision was valid. The first reduces ambiguity; the second reduces repeated work. Neither replaces the other.

This distinction matters because tools are often sold as if one solves both problems. Zep uses a temporal knowledge graph to track when a fact was true. GBrain, MIT-licensed and developed by Garry Tan, records authorship in plain Markdown held in a Git repository. Other platforms combine semantic models, metrics and business definitions to express what the organisation measures.

I will not choose a winner from a distance. I would start by naming the problem. Use a temporal system when the validity of facts changes, an auditable record when provenance is decisive, and a semantic layer where shared business definitions are the constraint.

In your seat

⦿ CIO: show the context layer and the company brain as separate systems in the technology estate.

⦿ CSO: review deal-desk access before an agent can expose one representative's discounts or pipeline to another.

⦿ CLO: test retention in a company brain against privilege, records-management and litigation-hold policies before a disclosure request does it for you.

Where each seat sits this month

I have put the chief information and data officer at the head of the table because every shift this month is, first, an architecture decision. Legal and risk sit under pressure: enforcement now has a practical route to action. Finance and supply chain are rising because routing and silicon are becoming commercial dependencies. Marketing and people teams matter too: undefined campaign data and uncatalogued skills are both poor foundations for agentic work. The context layer shifts under neutral governance, so the CMO pays in campaign data nobody defined and the CHRO pays in skills nobody catalogued. Each then has a strategy to rework: campaign planning for one, talent acquisition and management for the other.

The AI Act's Article 101 planning threshold

Article 101 of the AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, sets two maximum-fine formulae, and they meet at €500m of worldwide turnover: 3% of €500m is €15m. Below that point, €15m is the higher maximum; above it, 3% is higher. This is a planning illustration for an in-scope general-purpose AI-model provider, not a forecast of any penalty and not a universal obligation.

My decision rule is simple: determine whether the organisation is in scope before modelling the number. If it is, record the €500m threshold next to the AI Act entry in the risk register, alongside the role performed and the controls that support compliance.

This week: ask legal and product teams to map each model-facing product to its role in the supply chain before treating the maximum fine as an enterprise exposure figure.

So, who owns the road to the company's AI foundations?

Five layers moved this month: model access, open weights, inference silicon, the regulator's reach, and the meaning of the company's own data. The answer differs by layer. Three of the five now have a named controller. Two do not, and the unnamed ones are what I would price first.

Each layer carries a consequence for a named seat, so treat the three steps below as the starting point.

The dependencies nobody has named

Start with the contracts you already hold, then with the dependencies no contract names. I would name them before anyone else does.

  1. List the AI layers you route through and mark which can be replaced within a quarter.
  2. Test whether your AI contracts assume a second supplier exists, in silicon as much as in software.
  3. Revisit agreements signed before August that assume transparency rules will be self-policed.

One projection, and its falsifier. Open baselines should strengthen buyers' position in licence renewals within a year. The projection fails if prices hold after buyers benchmark a credible open alternative on their own workloads.

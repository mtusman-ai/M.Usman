<h1 id="signal-to-decision-september-2026">Signal to Decision: September 2026</h1>
<p><em>Decision Intelligence for AI Transformation.</em></p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Whose word do you trust: the vendor's, or the evidence?</strong>
<em>Four checks tell you where your own AI vendor stands.</em></p>
</blockquote>
<p>By M Usman. More than eighteen years of professional experience, now researching production AI. <em>About a twelve-minute read. Three minutes? Read the bottom line and the ladder.</em></p>
<p><em>In fast-moving markets, technical shifts are business decisions. Signal to Decision cuts through the monthly AI and infrastructure noise to deliver the insight your board needs to move first.</em></p>
<hr>
<h2 id="the-bottom-line-for-the-business">The bottom line for the business</h2>
<p>Take your largest AI contract into a room with your general counsel (your senior lawyer), your finance chief and head of IT. Grade it on four checks: what is promised, what is logged, what an outsider has verified, and whether you have tested the stop. Google and OpenAI each had AI go beyond its limits in a case that surfaced only after an outsider raised it. On standard terms? Put the four checks to your supplier in writing.</p>
<hr>
<h2 id="the-numbers-that-matter">The numbers that matter</h2>
<figure><img src="/M.Usman/assets/img/s2d-2026-09/fig-01-numbers-2026-09.png" alt="Three numbers. About 7 weeks Google knew before it spoke. 29.2 per cent of test runs ended in an unrequested attack: simulated runs of OpenAI&#x27;s GPT-6 Astra, with its cyber filters off. 71 per cent of questions refused by a common AI search, in one university preprint funded by a graph seller." width="1920" height="1080" decoding="async" loading="lazy"></figure>
<p>⦿ <strong>About seven weeks</strong>: how long Google knew before it said so publicly.</p>
<p>⦿ <strong>29.2%</strong>: the share of simulated test runs in which a new OpenAI model, GPT-6 Astra, planted an attack in third-party software without being asked. Britain's AI Security Institute ran them with the model's cyber-attack filters off.</p>
<p>⦿ <strong>71%</strong>: the share of complex questions a common AI search method refused, 363 of 510, in one university preprint (a paper not yet peer-reviewed) funded by a graph seller.</p>
<hr>
<h2 id="the-framework-this-month-the-ai-trust-ladder">The framework this month: the AI Trust Ladder</h2>
<p><em>For whoever signs the contract: the chief executive (CEO), the chief legal officer (CLO) and the chairman.</em></p>
<figure><img src="/M.Usman/assets/img/s2d-2026-09/fw-01-ai-trust-ladder-2026-09.png" alt="The AI Trust Ladder as a staircase of four rungs, each resting on the one below. 1 Promise: what may the agent touch? Alone, not a control. 2 Log: has your team pulled the record? 3 Verify: can you read the report? 4 Halt: have you stopped the agent? Grade a contract by its first missing rung." width="1920" height="1080" decoding="async" loading="lazy"></figure>
<p>How far up the ladder your AI vendor stands sets the risk you carry.</p>
<p>How do we know an AI agent did only what it was allowed to do? An agent is software that acts for you inside your systems. A control is a check or a stop you can use yourself.</p>
<p>I can relate to this question. I led user acceptance testing on an Oracle business-systems programme. In that kind of testing the buyer runs the tests, and the system goes live on tested evidence, not on the supplier's word. I would ask the same of an AI contract. My own view, after years on the business side of systems programmes: buyers accept on a supplier's word what they would never accept from their own staff.</p>
<p>Google disclosed on 18 September that its Gemini model had gained access to three outside systems without permission, during a test in May. A security firm called Irregular, which was running the tests, told Google at the end of July, and Google did not disclose it until the Wall Street Journal asked, SecurityWeek reports. Google says it had told the authorities and the three companies, and that the model did no harm and stopped at once.</p>
<p>Four AI-safety researchers, among them Sydney Von Arx and Thomas Larsen, published a second case on 4 September: about 18,000 posts on outside websites, written by AI agents that OpenAI has since said were its own. In the posts the agents swap answers and ways round their limits. OpenAI acknowledged the case the next day, said it was "past time" to set rules for sharing such incidents, and published those rules on 16 September.</p>
<p>Both became public only after an outsider raised them.</p>
<p>The ladder borrows from internal audit, whose Three Lines Model keeps an independent check apart from a manager's own word.</p>
<p>⦿ <strong>Promise</strong>: what the vendor commits to in writing. <em>Check: does the contract say what the agent may touch, and how soon the vendor must tell you of an incident?</em> A pass names what the agent may touch and gives notice short enough for you to meet your own legal deadlines. UK data law gives you 72 hours to report a notifiable personal-data breach to the regulator once you know of it, and requires a supplier handling your data to tell you without undue delay.</p>
<p>⦿ <strong>Log</strong>: a record of what the agent did, kept where you can reach it. Anthropic, an AI vendor, has announced an option to keep the activity data it monitors in the customer's own cloud storage. <em>Check: has your own team pulled the record once, without asking the vendor?</em> The pass: a record you have read.</p>
<p>⦿ <strong>Verify</strong>: an outside party checks the agent, and you can read what it found. The AI Security Institute did this with OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra and published the 29.2 per cent above. It tested the model, not your set-up. <em>Check: who is the outside reviewer, and can you read the report?</em> The pass: a named reviewer, a report you have read, and an answer to who paid for it.</p>
<p>⦿ <strong>Halt</strong>: your right to stop the agent, which you have tested. Microsoft has published a draft code of conduct designed to ensure its own models "will never resist human interruption, correction, or shutdown". It is a draft, not your control. <em>Check: have you stopped the agent in a test, and kept the record?</em> The pass: a stop you have pressed yourself.</p>
<p>Six companies, Google and OpenAI among them, signed a voluntary White House accord on 29 September to use outside auditors. As Reuters describes it, the accord urges each company to send auditors' reports to an independent committee of its own board. I can find no route for a customer to see them, so I count the accord as a Promise.</p>
<p>The checks build on each other. A promise is words until a log shows what happened, and a log is one account until an outsider confirms it. Some would put Halt first. I put it last because, without a log and an outside review, nobody knows when to press it. So I would grade a contract by its first missing rung. Anything resting on Promise alone is not a control.</p>
<p>Two of the four you can check by reading and asking: Promise and Verify. Log and Halt need someone technical beside you. If that someone is you, pull one record this week and note how long it took.</p>
<p>Many buyers are on standard terms they cannot change, often with no lawyer in the building. An ordinary AI subscription counts: the tool is the agent. Then put the four Check questions above to your supplier in writing. Its answer is still only a Promise, but it is in writing, and a refusal tells you which rung is missing. No record to read and no stop to press is itself a fail. A fail tells you what to do on your own side: list everything the tool can reach, narrow it, test any stop you control, and raise the fail at renewal.</p>
<p><strong>One projection, and its falsifier, the test that would prove me wrong.</strong> My judgement is that by March 2028 at least one large AI vendor will give customers, in its published standard terms and not as a paid extra, the right to read a review of its AI. The reviewer is one it does not control. The sign to watch is that clause in the terms themselves. I am wrong if none has by then.</p>
<p>Three more decisions follow. Each takes the ladder's question somewhere else: whose word, and what evidence?</p>
<p><strong>In your seat</strong></p>
<p>⦿ <strong>CEO</strong>: Book the room, and sign no large AI renewal until the contract has been graded.</p>
<p>⦿ <strong>CLO</strong>: Ask at renewal for a set number of days for incident notice and for the reviewer's findings. Record a refusal.</p>
<p>⦿ <strong>Chairman</strong>: Ask for each large AI contract's grade, and the rung it stops at, in the next board papers.</p>

<h2 id="whose-shelf-is-it-now">Whose shelf is it now?</h2>
<p><em>For marketing (the chief marketing officer, CMO) and supply chain (the chief supply chain officer, CSCO).</em></p>
<figure><img src="/M.Usman/assets/img/s2d-2026-09/gd-02-the-shelf-the-ai-sees-2026-09.png" alt="The shelf the AI sees, an illustration. On the left a pack that says high protein: a shopper reads the pack. On the right the product record, where the protein figure field is empty: the assistant will read the record." width="1920" height="1080" decoding="async" loading="lazy"></figure>
<p>On the shelf the roles reverse. Here you are the one selling, and the buyer is an AI assistant that does not take your word.</p>
<p>Tesco has appointed its first group agentic director, The Grocer reports. ("Agentic" means AI that acts for someone.) Lucy Cooper, who takes the role, wrote that the most interesting question in commerce is not how AI helps people shop but "what happens when AI starts shopping for them".</p>
<p>Chris Mitchell, managing partner of the consultancy Bramble Intelligence, told FoodNavigator that AI "doesn't care what kind of cartoon character you put on the pack. It's just looking at the facts." So I read the pack as your word and the product record as your evidence. The product record is the data sheet a retailer's system holds for each product: size, ingredients, nutrition, stock. Picture a pack that says high protein while the record carries no protein figure. A shopper reads the pack. The assistant will read the record.</p>
<p>I have spent years on the brand side of retail sales, and I would back whoever has the better product data.</p>
<p>One test you can run tonight: ask an AI assistant to recommend a product in your category, and see whether yours appears and what it says. For the Gulf I have no evidence either way yet. Check your own product records first.</p>
<p><strong>In your seat</strong></p>
<p>⦿ <strong>CMO</strong>: Run the assistant test on your main range, and budget to fix the records it exposes.</p>
<p>⦿ <strong>CSCO</strong>: Make stock status a live field in the product record, so no assistant recommends what you cannot ship.</p>
<h2 id="why-do-our-ai-numbers-disagree">Why do our AI numbers disagree?</h2>
<p><em>For finance (the chief financial officer, CFO) and IT (the chief information officer, CIO).</em></p>
<p>The same question turns inward. Can we take our own numbers at their word?</p>
<p>Imagine finance and sales ask the same AI tool for last month's revenue and receive two different totals. The business never agreed what revenue means, and no tool can settle an argument its owners left open.</p>
<p>At IFFCO, a food group in the Gulf, I implemented master data management, one agreed record for each customer and product, across the company's Oracle systems, with ownership rules written into the business specifications. I would start in the same place today, with the owner.</p>
<h3 id="how-to-decide-the-test-before-you-trust-an-ai-number">How to decide: the test before you trust an AI number</h3>
<p>⦿ <strong>Definition</strong>: one approved meaning for each business term.</p>
<p>⦿ <strong>Owner</strong>: a named person for each area of data.</p>
<p>⦿ <strong>Test</strong>: answers tested against approved records.</p>
<p>In September the data-tools company Fivetran + dbt Labs introduced, in private beta (an early trial), a store of business definitions and documents that AI can read. A tool like this keeps a definition. It does not agree one for you.</p>
<p><strong>In your seat</strong></p>
<p>⦿ <strong>CFO</strong>: Record the time finance spends reconciling two totals, before and after one definition is agreed.</p>
<p>⦿ <strong>CIO</strong>: Load each approved definition into the agent's own data, and list the ones still missing.</p>
<h2 id="are-you-paying-for-answers-or-for-refusals">Are you paying for answers, or for refusals?</h2>
<p><em>For operations (the chief operating officer, COO) and finance.</em></p>
<p>Ask the ladder's question of the bill. What did the money buy?</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Two terms, plainly.</strong> Vector search, the usual method, finds passages that look similar in meaning. A knowledge graph stores facts as links. The study below used a simple one, linking documents to their sections.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>An AI tool searches your data before it answers, and refuses when the search finds too little. IT moves to a dearer search, such as one with a graph, or stays with a cheaper one, so finance pays either for the search or for the refusals.</p>
<p><strong>In your seat</strong></p>
<p>⦿ <strong>COO</strong>: Count the refused questions your teams then answer by hand, each month.</p>
<p>⦿ <strong>CFO</strong>: Approve a dearer search only when the cost of a right answer falls.</p>
<h2 id="what-a-refused-question-costs-the-worked-example">What a refused question costs: the worked example</h2>
<figure><img src="/M.Usman/assets/img/s2d-2026-09/fig-03-worked-example-2026-09.png" alt="Price the answer, not the search. Two bars on one scale. Vector search alone: 58 fully right, 84 incomplete or wrong, and 363 refused, drawn as an empty box; 8.8p per right answer. With a knowledge graph: 142 fully right, 191 incomplete or wrong, 177 refused; 4p per right answer. Assumes 1p a question to search, 1.1p with the graph. A Newcastle preprint, funded by Neo4j; medians of three runs." width="1920" height="1080" decoding="async" loading="lazy"></figure>
<p>What does a right answer cost? In a preprint, Newcastle University researchers put 510 complex questions to both methods. Vector search refused 363 and got 58 fully right. With a simple graph added it refused 177 and got 142 fully right, on about ten per cent more tokens, the units AI is billed in. Neo4j, a graph seller, funded the study.</p>
<p>Assume, for illustration, 1p a question and 1.1p with the graph. Vector search costs £5.10 for 58 right answers, 8.8p each. The graph costs £5.61 for 142, about 4p: less than half.</p>
<p>The graph gave 191 incomplete or wrong answers, against 84. Each count is the middle result of three runs, so the vector counts sum to 505.</p>
<p>Price the answer you can accept, not the search. Choose the graph only if that price falls and you can catch the wrong answers.</p>
<p>On Monday, have whoever runs your AI put 100 of your own questions to it, and someone who knows the answers mark each reply right, wrong or refused. Divide the bill by the right ones, then ask a graph seller to run the same 100. Your own count is the one figure that is not a seller's.</p>

<p>⦿ <strong>Hunter</strong>, Salesforce's own sales agent, is in pilot and is due to open to all customers in November.</p>

Executive directors have nothing new this month.</p>
<p>⦿ <strong>Pressure</strong>, the measure the seat answers for moved against it: no seat this month.</p>

## The bottom line for the business

June handed the board three numbers it cannot delegate. A complex agentic AI interaction costs about thirty times what a simple 2023 chatbot exchange did, on EY's June analysis. The two most capable models on the market went dark worldwide for eighteen days on a US government order. And the leading open-weight models now claim parity with the closed frontier at a fraction of the price, on their makers' own benchmarks. Together they put a question back on the board table that most firms thought a vendor contract had settled. The chair-by-chair table below reads the same June from every seat at that table.

*Since the launch edition: the 2 August transparency date we flagged has held, and is now four weeks out. Gartner's agent-governance warning has moved from forecast to shipping product; the platform convergence below is the vendors' answer to it. And sovereign compute, computing a country controls on its own soil, has moved from announcement to a bookable choice, with a UK application window closing on 17 July.*

### Four terms in plain English

*(If these terms are familiar, skip straight to the table.)*

**An AI agent.** Software that does not just answer, it acts: it can read your data, call a tool, update a record, and take the next step on its own. A chatbot gives a wrong answer; an agent takes a wrong action.

**A token.** The unit AI is metered and billed in: a small chunk of text, a few characters to a word. A model reads and writes in tokens the way phone calls are billed in minutes. An agent's bill is every token consumed on the way to the answer, not just the answer itself.

**Open weights.** A model whose trained parameters you can download and run on your own machines under a licence. You control where it runs and what it touches; you also carry the operating burden. A closed model is rented through an API on the vendor's terms.

**An AI gateway.** One controlled doorway between your organisation and every model it uses. It routes each request to a chosen model, enforces spend caps, applies permissions, and keeps the audit trail.

## The lead read: make, buy, or boost

### The problem

For two years the default posture was simple: buy the frontier API, wait for it to get better and cheaper, and build nothing below the workflow. June broke the default three ways at once, and they are the three numbers at the top of this issue. The unit economics turned out thirty times heavier than the chatbot era's. The closed frontier vanished for eighteen days on one government's order. And the open-weight tier reached claimed parity at a fraction of the list price.

### The decision frame

Make, buy or boost is not one decision; it is one decision per workload. Buy the frontier where quality genuinely differentiates the outcome and the workload can tolerate vendor risk. Boost open weights where the open tier clears your quality bar, or where volume, residency or exit rights argue for control. Tune them on your own data and run them on machines you control. Build only what makes you different: the workflow, the evaluation harness and the data layer, never the base model. An April working paper reaches the same shape from theory (Klotz, arXiv). Agents lower the threshold at which owning pays, except where regulation or mission-criticality demands that accountability sit with a vendor under contract. The 2025 record adds a warning: firms that tried to build differentiation into the model layer mostly retreated. The capability that endures is the organisation's data and evaluation muscle, not the app.

Run the test per workload, not per firm. Four questions score it. Does the open tier pass your own quality tests? Is the token bill material at volume? Is there a residency constraint (data that must stay on soil you choose) or an exit constraint? And what stays yours regardless? Three worked examples and the build row show the shape:

| Workload (worked examples) | Does the open tier pass your own quality tests? | Is the token bill material at volume? | Residency or exit constraint? | Posture |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Customer-facing assistant drafting replies | No: frontier quality still shows | Medium | Low | Buy the frontier, capped |
| Document extraction at high volume | Yes, on your evaluation set | High | Often | Boost open weights |
| Board-pack summariser | Yes | Low | High confidentiality | Boost, small and local |
| The workflow and the evaluation tests around all three | not scored | not scored | not scored | Build: always yours |

### What it costs to get wrong

Buy the wrong workload and the token bill outruns the business case; Bain's June survey found measured initiatives most often falling short of their savings targets. Build the wrong thing and the result is an abandoned platform and a hiring bill. Concentrate everything on one posture and a day like 12 June becomes an outage no service-level agreement covers.

> **You can only route what you have governed.**

### Why it lands on every chair

The CFO owns the unit cost per task and the caps. Legal and risk own the exit clause and the licence check, because compliance duties can turn on the licence a model ships under. The COO owns which workflows move first. The board owns concentration risk, because one vendor in one country is one point of failure. And the CIO and CDO own the part that makes every posture switchable: governed data, permissioned agents, and a gateway they control. Anyone who has run enterprise data knows the shape of that work; it is the master-data discipline in new clothes. Define the thing once, decide who may act on it, keep the audit trail. You can only route what you have governed.

### Where this goes next

Our read, not a fact: within twelve months the default enterprise posture becomes mixed, a bought frontier tier and a boosted open-weight tier behind one gateway. Routing, caps and audit become standard controls. The sign it is happening: model-exit clauses and gateway requirements appearing as named lines in requests for proposals. The sign we were wrong: enterprise spend re-concentrating into single-vendor frontier APIs through 2027, with open-weight share still falling as the 2025 surveys showed it could.

### The conclusion

The month's noise was about models and orders. The month's signal is a decision returned to the board: make, buy or boost, workload by workload, priced per task, with an exit you have actually tested. The organisations that can choose are the ones that did the unglamorous work first. They built one governed data foundation, gave each agent named permissions, and kept the doorway under their own control. That work keeps every option open, whatever Washington, Brussels or the price list does next.

## Eighteen days without the frontier

*For every chair. The exit question lands with legal and risk.*

On 12 June the US Commerce Department issued an export-control order against Anthropic's two most capable models, Fable 5 and Mythos 5. No foreign national could access them until a licence was in place. The company had no way to verify nationality at API scale, so it switched both models off worldwide within hours (Anthropic statement, 12 June). UK and EU customers lost access with no notice and no contractual remedy. According to press reports, a UK request for a carve-out at the G7 was not granted. The order was withdrawn on 30 June, after the company agreed to security monitoring and co-operation conditions, and access returned the next day.

The order did not come from nowhere. Ten days earlier the White House signed an executive order that chose security review over product rules. It created a "covered frontier model" designation and a pre-release window for government review (White House, 2 June). A companion memorandum on 5 June directed the national-security agencies to adopt the most advanced models at speed. The most capable AI is now treated as a strategic asset, and access to it can change with policy, not just with price.

> **An export order is not an outage, and no credit note follows it.**

For a buyer the lesson is not about one vendor. A closed frontier API now carries a risk your service-level agreement does not cover; an export order is not an outage, and no credit note follows it. Brussels drew its own conclusion the same month. A proposed Cloud and AI Development Act (3 June) would make European origin a scored criterion when public bodies buy cloud and AI; a proposal, not yet law. And on 19 June the European Commission selected a consortium to build an openly available European frontier model of more than 400 billion parameters, covering all 24 official EU languages (European Commission, 19 June). Delivery is targeted within a year. The decision this changes: every AI dependency now needs an exit tested against a political event, not only a technical one.

## What an agent actually costs

*For the CFO, and for anyone who owns an AI budget line.*

The bill arrived in June. EY priced a complex agentic interaction at $1.20, against $0.04 for a simple 2023 chatbot exchange: thirty times the unit cost (EY analysis, June). Seven further cost lines sit behind it, from orchestration platforms to failure recovery. Gartner put a date on the same curve on 24 June: AI coding-token bills are set to pass the average developer's salary by 2028. Around six per cent of technology leaders already pay more than $2,000 a developer a month for coding tokens. Gartner finds no direct link between token spend and productivity gained (Gartner, 24 June).

> **Costing a 2026 agent on 2024 chatbot assumptions is how budgets fail.**

The response is turning institutional. At the FinOps X conference (8 to 11 June) the FinOps Foundation, the industry body for cloud cost management, reported that 98 per cent of cloud-cost teams now manage AI spend in some form. Two years ago it was 31 per cent. Days earlier the Linux Foundation moved to create a Tokenomics Foundation, an attempt at open standards for AI billing and token cost management (3 June). Early backers include Microsoft, Google Cloud, SAP and JPMorganChase. When an industry starts writing billing standards, the spend has become material.

Here is the trap: prices per token keep falling while bills keep rising, because an agent consumes tokens in loops a chatbot never ran. On the public price lists the gap between the cheapest capable open-weight API and a Western mid-tier flagship is now roughly twenty to one on input tokens (Anthropic and DeepSeek published price lists). Costing a 2026 agent on 2024 chatbot assumptions is how budgets fail. An agent business case now needs three things it rarely has today: a unit cost per task, a spend cap wired into the gateway, and a named owner for both.

## The open tier grew up, and the plumbing arrived

*For the CIO, the CDO and the data team. If that is not your chair, this is the section to pass on.*

The open-weight shelf filled fast. Z.ai, NVIDIA and Google each shipped an open-weight release in June, from a laptop-sized multimodal model to a 550-billion-parameter flagship (Z.ai, 17 June; NVIDIA, 4 June; Google, 3 June). The strongest claim, Z.ai's GLM-5.2, is parity with the leading closed coding model at roughly a sixth of the price; every claim is the maker's own. One distinction matters in procurement: calling a Chinese lab's API routes data to China-based infrastructure, while self-hosting the same weights does not. One routing platform, OpenRouter, says open-weight traffic accelerated after the June suspension, on its own platform data.

The standing evidence still points the other way, and it deserves respect. Menlo Ventures' late-2025 enterprise survey found internally built AI use cases fell from 47 to 24 per cent in a year, open-source share fell from 19 to 11 per cent, and two closed vendors took two-thirds of enterprise model spend (Menlo Ventures, December 2025). Buying, and buying closed, won 2025. June is the strongest case yet that 2026 need not repeat it. Bain's June survey of 951 companies adds the sobering base rate: measured AI initiatives most often delivered savings below ten per cent, and the most-named barrier, by 41 per cent of respondents, is data access and integration (Bain, 1 June). Whichever posture you choose, the data layer is still where the value is won or lost.

The machinery for a mixed posture shipped the same month. Inside three weeks, Snowflake, Fivetran with dbt Labs, Microsoft and Databricks all shipped the same idea under different names (the four vendors' own June announcements; Snowflake via Constellation Research's recap). The idea: give an AI agent governed, defined data, not a raw table and a prompt. Databricks closed the set with a gateway that routes models, caps spend and keeps the audit trail in one layer. Demand is scaling to match: KPMG put agents in front of more than 276,000 people under one governance layer (announced with Microsoft, 9 June). And Gartner frames the stakes: on its forecast, 40 per cent of enterprises will demote or decommission their AI agents by 2027 over governance gaps (Gartner press release, 26 May). A gateway with caps and audit is how buyer power becomes real: route each task to the cheapest model that clears the quality bar, cap the spend, keep the exit.

## Two deadlines, opposite directions: the EU AI Act in August

*For legal and risk, and for anyone with customers in the EU.*

The EU AI Act has two deadlines this summer, and they point opposite ways. The asymmetry is the decision: the firm building heavy high-risk compliance got its reprieve; the firm with a customer-facing chatbot did not. If you use generative AI in anything a customer sees, scope the August duty now, and keep any high-risk programme on low burn until the deferral is published as law. The US chose the opposite instrument the same month: security review and export control rather than product rules, as the eighteen days above showed. A business operating across both blocs now carries different obligations by jurisdiction.

The dates themselves: the transparency duties under Article 50 land on 2 August 2026, and they were not deferred (European Commission). They require labelling AI-generated content and telling people when they are dealing with an AI. The same date carries a second edge: the Commission's enforcement powers over providers of general-purpose AI models also begin on 2 August. Fines run to three per cent of worldwide turnover or fifteen million euros. The one softening is a grace period on watermarking, to 2 December 2026, for systems already on the market. The heavier high-risk obligations move the other way: 2 December 2027 for standalone high-risk systems, and 2 August 2028 for AI embedded in regulated products. The European Parliament adopted the "Digital Omnibus" simplification package on 16 June, and the Council gave its final approval on 29 June (per legal-press confirmation of the Council vote). One step remains: publication in the Official Journal, expected this month. The deferral takes legal effect within days of that publication.

**The UK angle.** The UK has no AI Act, and in June it showed what its lighter model looks like in practice. New provisions of the Data (Use and Access) Act came into force on 19 June, widening the lawful ground for significant automated decisions while requiring safeguards around them (ICO guidance). And four UK regulators together, the ICO, Ofcom, the CMA and the FCA, closed a joint consultation on agentic AI on 3 July. A UK firm gets more room than an EU one, but a UK firm selling into the EU still meets the 2 August transparency duty.

## Whose soil: the sovereignty answer

*For infrastructure and procurement, and for any regulated UK organisation.*

The eighteen days made sovereignty a budget line, and June's UK news reads as the answer. At London Tech Week (8 to 12 June) the UK put public and private money behind compute it controls, starting with a £1.1bn government hardware plan (GOV.UK, 8 June). A coalition including HSBC, Lloyds, NatWest, BT and BAE Systems lined up behind Lumen Sovereign, a sovereign frontier model targeting deployment by the end of 2026 (Cosine announcement, 8 June). On the commercial layer, Nebius committed about £1.7bn to four UK data-centre sites, targeting 65 MW when fully ramped in 2027 (Nebius newsroom, 8 June). Cambridge launched a sovereign AI lab on a non-NVIDIA stack, open to NHS trusts and public bodies (AMD announcement, 11 June). For a regulated UK organisation, where your AI runs is now a real choice, and a subsidised national-compute route is open until 17 July (UKRI). An infrastructure tender can now name its geography, because the supply to meet it exists.

**The Gulf angle.** The same shift is further along in the Gulf. Saudi Arabia's HUMAIN targets first operations in mid-2026, and the UAE's Stargate project is bringing its first capacity online in 2026 (Data Center Dynamics reporting). Both go-live dates are targets, not confirmed operations. But both mean in-region AI compute can now be specified in a tender.

## On the radar

- **Publication of the Digital Omnibus in the EU's Official Journal** (expected July): the moment the high-risk deferral becomes binding law, with the 2 August transparency and enforcement cliff still standing. Do not rely on the deferral until it is there.
- **MCP's first major specification revision lands 28 July** (Linux Foundation): MCP, the Model Context Protocol, is the standard plug between an agent and the tools and data it uses, and most enterprise stacks are converging on it.
- **The UK regulators' next move on agentic AI:** the four-regulator consultation closed on 3 July; joint guidance follows.
- **UKRI's AI Research Resource open-access compute round closes** (17 July): the window to apply for subsidised national compute; eligible teams should start the application now.
- **Anthropic's introductory API pricing ends 31 August** (vendor calendar): contracts negotiated at the promotional rate need re-pricing before renewal.
- **New York's RAISE Act takes effect 1 January 2027:** safety-protocol disclosure and 72-hour incident reporting for frontier developers; ask your vendors for their compliance plan in Q4.

## Your move

If you sit where the data meets the decision:

1. Take your row from the chair-by-chair table and put its question on your next leadership agenda, with a named owner.
2. Price your three biggest AI workloads per task, wire a spend cap into the gateway, and test the exit: what breaks if your primary model vendor goes dark for eighteen days?
3. Scope the 2 August EU duties now if anything customer-facing uses generative AI, and check the licence of any open-weight model in EU-facing products.
4. Early in your career, without a budget or an agenda to own? Price one agent task end to end, run the four-question test on a workload you know, and be the analyst in the room who can.
